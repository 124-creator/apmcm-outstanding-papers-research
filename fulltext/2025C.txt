2025 APMCM summary sheet
The implementation of the “Reciprocal Tariff” policy marks a paradigm shift from efficiency-driven
globalization to security-driven fragmentation. To quantify the cascading effects of this policy, this
paper constructs a Systemic Multi-Dimensional Evaluation Framework (SMDEF) that integrates
econometrics, game theory, and system dynamics across agricultural, industrial, and macroeconomic
layers.
Micro-Level Impacts: Supply Chain Restructuring
• Agriculture (Irreversible Displacement, Q1): Utilizing a Constrained Armington Model,
we reveal a structural break. A 25% tariff triggers a 64.1% collapse in U.S. soybean exports to
China. Crucially, our model captures Brazil’s physical capacity saturation, identifying Argentina
as the unexpected strategic beneficiary with a 245% export surge.
• Automotive (Forced Localization, Q2): Through a Dynamic Stackelberg Game, we identify a
precise Tariff Tipping Point of 11.1%. Beyond this threshold, the cost of direct import exceeds
local production, forcing Japanese automakers into a “Forced Localization” strategy, effectively
trading consumer surplus for U.S. manufacturing jobs.
• Semiconductors (Innovation Dilemma, Q3): Our Bi-level Policy Simulation demonstrates
that tariffs alone fail to bridge the 40% manufacturing cost gap. Subsidies are a necessary
condition for reshoring. Moreover, aggressive export controls create a “Boomerang Effect”,
stripping U.S. firms of 30% of revenue and eroding long-term innovation budgets.
Macro-Level Insights: Fiscal Limits & Systemic Risk
• The Fiscal Paradox (Q4): We debunk the myth of linear revenue growth. Our Crisis-Aware
Forecasting confirms a Laffer Curve effect, pinpointing the Optimal Tariff Rate at≈30%.
The proposed 60% rate lies in the “Over-taxation Zone”, accelerating tax base erosion and trade
diversion (leakage to Vietnam/Mexico).
• The Stagflation Trap (Q5): The Supply-Side Shock Model proves that reciprocal tariffs, when
met with targeted retaliation (e.g., Critical Minerals Ban), trigger a Stagflationary Trap. The
surge in input costs and interest rates neutralizes the protective benefits of tariffs, rendering the
“Reshoring” goal economically unfeasible.
The Synthesis of the Systemic Framework
Our SMDEF framework (integrating cross-layer quantitative models) confirms the “Reciprocal Tariff”
strategy’s structural flaws. Two paradoxes emerge: it violates the Laffer Curve optimum (≈30%)
to accelerate fiscal erosion, and triggers a Stagflationary Trap via higher input costs/interest rates
(Q5). Short-term forced localization gains (11.1% tipping point) are offset by systemic losses, making
the policy a long-term Negative-Sum Game. True economic resilience thus requires shifting from
distortive border taxes to a policy of fiscal prudence and targeted domestic industrial subsidies.
Keywords: Systemic Evaluation Framework; Constrained Armington Model; Dynamic Stackelberg
Game; Laffer Curve Optimization; Stagflationary Trap;Supply Chain Resilience

I. Introduction
1 . 1 Background
On April 2, 2025, the U.S. Trump administration launched the “Reciprocal Tariffs” policy, imposing
a 10% minimum baseline tariff on all trading partners and additional higher tariffs on 60 trade-deficit
economies (including China). The U.S. claimed this would cut the trade deficit and boost manufactur-
ing reshoring, but the policy violated WTO “Most-Favored-Nation Treatment” and developing-country
tariff preferences. By August 2025, the U.S. trade-weighted average tariff surged from 2.44% (early
2025) to 20.11%—its highest since 1933. While WTO-permitted tariffs can protect domestic in-
dustries, excessive tariffs disrupt global trade and long-term growth. In the short term, U.S. high
tariffs increased revenue and narrowed the trade deficit but also caused export declines and domestic
inflation. GACC data showed U.S. imports from China fell 20% in the policy’s first month (with U.S.
exports to China down 13% amid Chinese countermeasures), by May 2025, these declines widened
to 35% and 18%, weighing on U.S. industries.
1 . 2 Question Restatement
Using U.S. tariff and global trade data, we analyze the impact of U.S. tariff adjustments on international
trade and regional economies, addressing:
Question 1 Analyze China’s soybean trade with the U.S., Brazil, and Argentina (the world’s top
soybean producers/exporters). Model the impact of U.S. tariff adjustments on their soybean industries
and estimate post-adjustment export volume/value distribution.
Question 2 Analyze Japanese automobiles’ position in the U.S. market (where 46% of 2024 car
sales were imported). Model the impact of U.S. tariff adjustments on U.S.-Japan auto trade, U.S. auto
import structure, and the U.S. auto industry, incorporating Japan’s non-tariff responses and economic
transmission effects.
Question 3 Model the effects of U.S. tariff policies on domestic semiconductor manufacturing
and high/mid/low-end chip trade, considering economic efficiency and national security (noting the
U.S. leads in semiconductors but lacks strong manufacturing, and imposed high-end chip export con-
trols on China).
Question 4 Model the short/medium-term impact of U.S. tariff adjustments on U.S. tariff revenue
and predict the net change during Trump’s second term.
Question 5 Model the short/medium-term impact of U.S. tariff adjustments on the U.S. economy
(considering trading partners’ countermeasures like China’s reciprocal tariffs and rare earth/lithium
battery export controls). Evaluate if “reciprocal tariffs” can truly drive manufacturing reshoring.

firms maximize profits; and consumers maximize utility under budget constraints.

III. Data Preprocessing and Descriptive Analysis
3 . 1 Data Sources
To ensure the empirical validity of our models, we aggregated high-frequency trade and economic
data from multiple authoritative sources. The dataset covers the period from January 2020 to May
2025, providing a solid baseline for forecasting.
3 . 2 Data Processing Framework
Raw trade data often contains noise, reporting lags, and inconsistencies. We implemented a three-stage
preprocessing pipeline:
1. Outlier Detection & Removal: We applied the Interquartile Range (IQR) method to identify
and filter out anomalous trade spikes caused by one-off events (e.g., port strikes) that do not
reflect structural trends.
2. Missing Value Imputation: For minor gaps in monthly data (e.g., late reporting from Argentina),
we used Linear Interpolation. For longer gaps, we employed Seasonal Decomposition of Time
Series (STL) to impute values while preserving seasonal patterns (crucial for the soybean model
in Q1).
3. Harmonization: Trade flows reported by exporters (e.g., China exports to US) often differ from
importer reports (e.g., US imports from China) due to “CIF/FOB” differences and transit lags. We
standardized all values to Landed Cost (CIF) basis to ensure consistency in tariff calculations.
3 . 3 Rationality of Estimated Parameters
A key challenge in our modeling is forecasting future parameters (e.g., elasticity shifts, 2026-2028
prices) under extreme policy shocks. We justify our estimates as follows:
• Tariff Pass-Through Rate (≈ 100%): We assume tariffs are fully passed on to U.S. consumers.
This is supported by empirical studies on the 2018-2019 trade war (e.g., Amiti et al., 2019),
which found that U.S. domestic prices rose 1:1 with tariffs due to the specialized nature of
Chinese imports.
• Substitution Elasticity (𝜎): For commodities like soybeans, we set a high𝜎 = 5.0 based on
GTAP database estimates, reflecting high substitutability. For specialized goods like rare earths,
we set a low𝜎= 0.5, reflecting supply rigidity.
• Reaction Lags (18 months): In Q2 (Auto) and Q5 (Reshoring), we assume an 18-month lag for
capacity expansion. This is consistent with the average construction cycle of a new manufacturing
plant, preventing our model from predicting unrealistic “instantaneous” reshoring.

9 . 1 Main Conclusions

affordability.

9 . 3 Strengths, Weaknesses, and Future Directions
Strengths: Our framework effectively integrates sector-specific granularity (Armington/Game The-

(2020-2024).
Weaknesses: The current model relies on partial equilibrium assumptions, potentially overlooking
cross-sector substitution, and simplifies complex geopolitical motives into rational behaviors.
Future Work: Future research could integrate these modules into a Computable General Equi-
librium (CGE) framework to capture economy-wide spillovers, or employ Agent-Based Modeling
