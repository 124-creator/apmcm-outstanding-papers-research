PDMS thin films are suited for radiative cooling. This study employs spectral analy-
sis and adaptive optimization algorithms to comprehensively investigate their emissivity
and cooling performance, exploring the impact of film thickness, material selection, and
multilayer mold structure design on overall cooling efficiency and cost feasibility.
For question 1, the transfer matrix method (TMM) was used to compute the spec-
tral reflectance of PDMS films, and Kirchhoff’s law was applied to derive wavelength-
dependent emissivity. Model validation shows a solar-band average emissivity of
0.0389 and an atmospheric-window standard deviation of 0.1651, consistent with
physical expectations. Results indicate very low emissivity in the solar spectrum and
a marked increase in the atmospheric window with thickness.
In response to question 2, we assess the radiative cooling performance of PDMS
thin films using a weighted evaluation function that combines net cooling power,
atmospheric-window emissivity, and solar absorptivity. Grey relational analysis
was applied to dynamically determine indicator weights, emphasizing net cooling power
as the primary factor. The weights for net cooling power, atmospheric-window emis-
sivity, and solar absorptivity are 0.484, 0.333, and 0.183, respectively.We propose the
following three recommendations:the thickness of PDMS films should be controlled
within the range of 20-60 𝜇m;doping the PDMS matrix with high-refractive-index
nanoparticles;adopting multilayer structures or photonic crystal structures.
In addressing question 3, the optimization function was constructed by combining
the performance evaluation model and material cost function from question 2. Then,
an adaptive differential evolution algorithm was used to optimize the multilayer thin
film structure. The optimal solution is a three-layer structure of PDMS, SiO2, and
Ag, with thicknesses of 19.206𝜇m, 0.010𝜇m and 0.120𝜇m, respectively.
Regarding question 4, A porous PDMS/SiO2/Ag multilayer was optimized using
MG-EMT for microstructural modeling, TMM for spectral emissivity, and GRA for
multi-objective optimization of cooling power and lifecycle cost.The final design com-
bines a 24.6𝜇m porous PDMS layer(11.9% porosity) with Al2O3 protection, yielding
high emissivity, low solar absorption, and a low cost of $14.95 m−2.

1 . 2 Question Restatement
This study addresses a mathematical modeling problem focused on the analysis, evalu-
ation, and optimization of radiative cooling materials and structures.
1 . 2 . 1 Question 1
A mathematical model is required to characterize the spectral emissivity of PDMS
thin films as a function of wavelength for varying film thicknesses. This model aims
to quantify the influence of film thickness on emissivity and provide a theoretical
foundation for evaluating radiative cooling performance.
1 . 2 . 2 Question 2
A performance evaluation model is established to assess the radiative cooling effective-
ness of PDMS thin films with different thicknesses. The model should incorporate the
thermal radiation exchange through the atmospheric transparency window and quan-
tify the cooling potential under varying material configurations, providing guidance for
material selection and design.
1 . 2 . 3 Question 3
Based on the emissivity characteristics of PDMS, suitable additional materials are
selected to construct a multilayer film. An optimization model is then developed to
determine the appropriate material selection and layer thickness for each component,
aiming to maximize radiative cooling performance while considering practical feasibility
constraints.
1 . 2 . 4 Question 4
Building upon the previous tasks, an integrated optimization model is constructed to
achieve the optimal design of radiative cooling materials and multilayer structures.

II. Problem Analysis
2 . 1 Analysis of Question 1
This problem requires establishing a physical model to simulate the wavelength-dependent
reflectance and emissivity of a PDMS optical structure and to analyze how film thick-
ness influences its radiative behavior for passive cooling. Considering that the sys-
tem is governed by electromagnetic wave propagation in stratified media, the Transfer
Matrix Method (TMM) derived from Maxwell’s equations is introduced to describe
multilayer interference and to compute spectral reflectance 𝑅(𝜆,𝑑) and emissivity
𝜀(𝜆,𝑑)= 1−𝑅(𝜆,𝑑). With accurate emissivity evaluation across the solar (0.3–2.5𝜇m)
and mid-infrared (3–25𝜇m) bands as the objective and consistent optical-constant data
as the constraint, a full-spectrum computational model is constructed through pre-
processing, interpolation, and fusion of multisource refractive-index datasets. Then,
the reflectivity is solved using the TMM algorithm, and the emissivity spectrum and
wavelength–thickness mapping are obtained according to Kirchhoff’s laws, thereby
identifying key emission features and high-emissivity regions. Sensitivity checks on
solar-band transparency and mid-IR emissivity smoothness are finally used to validate
the physical correctness and robustness of the model.

2 . 3 Analysis of Question 3
Problem 3 focuses on designing a cost-effective multilayer radiative-cooling structure
by constructing a model that jointly evaluates optical performance and material cost.
Based on the transfer matrix method, the spectral reflectance and emissivity of arbi-
trary multilayer stacks are computed across the solar and mid-infrared ranges, enabling
quantitative evaluation of net cooling power, atmospheric-window emissivity, and solar
absorption. These metrics are integrated into a weighted performance function, to which
a cost-penalty term is added for balancing cooling efficiency and material expense. The
design variables include discrete material selections and continuous thickness parame-
ters; thus, an adaptive differential evolution algorithm is adopted for global optimization
of the mixed-variable space. By tuning the cost-penalty coefficient, the performance–
cost Pareto frontier is obtained, revealing the optimal configurations under different cost
constraints. The final results show that the PDMS/SiO2/Ag structure provides the best
trade-off between cooling performance and material cost.

III. Problem Assumptions
The following assumptions are made for the optical and thermal modeling of the PDMS
radiative cooling system:
• Wavelength Range: The spectral calculations cover the range from 0.3 𝜇m to
55.56 𝜇m, with particular emphasis on 0.3–2.5 𝜇m for solar absorption and 8–
13𝜇m for the atmospheric window.
• Optical Properties: Each layer is assumed to be planar, homogeneous, and
isotropic. Layer thicknesses can vary from a few nanometers to several microme-
ters depending on the material. Surface roughness is neglected, but can optionally
be included in a scattering model.
• Angle of Incidence: Optical properties are averaged over all angles, either through
angular integration or by approximating a Lambertian surface, assuming unpolar-
ized incident light.
• Temperature Effects: Convection and conduction losses are accounted for using
a linearized approximation with a heat transfer coefficientℎ𝑐.
• Atmosphere: Wavelength-dependent atmospheric transmittance 𝜏atm(𝜆) is ap-
plied, with all materials assumed to be under atmospheric pressure.
• Substrate Effect: Without a substrate, light would transmit directly through the
film, resulting in extremely low emissivity regardless of thickness. Therefore, a
Ag substrate is used. For sufficiently thick PDMS films in the infrared band, the
transmittance is assumed to be negligible,𝑇(𝜆)≈ 0.
• Normal Incidence Assumption: All spectral calculations are performed under
normal incidence to isolate and analyze the effect of PDMS film thickness on the
intrinsic optical properties of the system.

5 . 1 . 1 Data Preprocessing

5 . 1 . 2 Establishment of the TMM Model
Model Principle. The Transfer Matrix Method is a rigorous numerical model based on
Maxwell’s equations and thin-film optics theory. This method treats light propagation in
multilayer thin-film structures as the sequential multiplication of transfer matrices. By
calculating the reflection and transmission of light at each interface and accounting for
interference effects within each layer, TMM enables precise determination of the overall
reflectance and transmittance of the system. In this study, we use TMM to compute the
reflectance spectrum of PDMS thin films and subsequently derive the spectral emissivity
according to Kirchhoff’s law of thermal radiation.

thermal radiation.

investigated in this work is composed of four layers: air / PDMS / dielectric layer /
reflective layer.

The optimization algorithm employed in this study is the adaptive differential
evolution algorithm, a population-based global optimization method. Its core mech-
anism lies in dynamically adjusting the mutation factor and crossover probability to
balance exploration and exploitation capabilities. By simulating biological evolu-
tion processes—mutation, crossover, and selection—the algorithm conducts parallel
searches for the global optimum within the solution space. Compared with conventional
differential evolution, its adaptive variant automatically tunes the search strategy accord-
ing to population diversity: maintaining a relatively large mutation factor in the early
stage to enhance global exploration and avoid premature convergence, and reducing the
mutation factor in the later stage to strengthen local refinement and improve convergence
accuracy.

perspective; a design that appears cheaper initially due to simplified processing would
ultimately prove more costly due to rapid failure rates.

VI. Model evaluation
6 . 1 Advantages of the model
6 . 1 . 1 Accurate Calculation Results
The Transfer Matrix Method (TMM) enables accurate computation of the spectral
reflectance of multilayer films, from which wavelength-dependent emissivity can be de-
rived. Model validation (e.g., solar-band average emissivity of 0.0389 and atmospheric-
window standard deviation of 0.1651) aligns well with physical expectations, demon-
strating reliability.
6 . 1 . 2 Comprehensive and Holistic Evaluation
Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) enables multi-objective evaluation by weighting net
cooling power, atmospheric-window emissivity, and solar absorptivity. Unlike fixed-
weight methods, GRA dynamically determines the relative importance of each indicator,
allowing the model to adaptively emphasize the most critical factors, such as net cooling

6 . 1 . 3 Integration of Microstructural Modeling and Multi-Objective Optimization
MG-EMT (Maxwell-Garnett Effective Medium Theory) combined with TMM facilitates
accurate modeling of porous PDMS microstructures, and coupled with lifecycle cost
(LCA) considerations, enables high-performance, cost-effective design.
6 . 2 Disadvantages of the model
6 . 2 . 1 High Computational Complexity
The combination of TMM, MG-EMT, and DE optimization increases computational
cost, especially when considering multiple parameters such as thickness, porosity, and
material types.
6 . 2 . 2 Idealized Assumptions Do Not Match Practical Applications
This model primarily considers standard atmospheric conditions and an ideal solar spec-
trum, and may not fully reflect cooling performance under different climatic conditions.
Furthermore, MG-EMT homogenizes the porous structure, potentially neglecting the
effects of pore shape and non-uniform distribution. TMM assumes smooth interfaces
and uniform thickness, which may not fully reflect deviations in actual manufacturing
processes.
6 . 2 . 3 Practical Fabrication Constraints
Optimized structures may be challenging to fabricate in practice, reducing practical
applicability of the model results.
